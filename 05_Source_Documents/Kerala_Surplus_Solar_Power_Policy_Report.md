## GOVERNMENT OF KERALA

POLICY REPORT

## Effective Utilisation of Surplus Solar Power in Kerala

Strategies for energy storage, flexible demand, smart-grid operation

and productive use of excess renewable electricity

| Submitted to | Government of Kerala |
| --- | --- |
| Subject | Managing periods when solar generation exceeds electricity demand |
| Document type | Technical and policy proposal |

## Core policy principle

Generate renewable electricity when sunlight is available; use it directly where possible; store or convert the surplus when immediate demand is low; and export remaining power when technically and commercially feasible.

Prepared as a proposal for consideration. All project capacities and allocations in this report are illustrative and require detailed feasibility, grid-impact, environmental and financial studies before approval.


## Executive Summary

Kerala's growing solar capacity creates an opportunity to reduce dependence on conventional electricity sources. It also creates a system-management challenge: at certain hours, solar generation may exceed local demand or the grid's ability to absorb it. This can lead to curtailment, even when electricity is needed later in the day.

The recommended response is not simply to install more solar panels. Kerala should build an integrated system that combines battery storage, pumped hydro storage, flexible electricity demand, electric-vehicle charging, industrial load shifting, smart-grid controls and interstate power trading. Green hydrogen can be developed selectively as a longer-term option where there is a credible industrial offtake.

The objective is to maximise the useful value of renewable generation while maintaining grid security, affordability and environmental safeguards.

## 1. Context and Problem Definition

A surplus can occur at different timescales. Annual energy surplus means total annual generation exceeds annual consumption. Hourly surplus occurs when generation at a particular time is greater than demand and available export or storage capacity. A state may therefore experience daytime solar curtailment and still need to purchase electricity during evening peak hours.

Policy planning should use hourly or sub-hourly data—not annual totals alone—to identify where, when and why surplus occurs. The first step should be a transparent solar-generation, demand, transmission and curtailment assessment.

## 3. Policy Objectives

- Reduce avoidable renewable-energy curtailment.

- Shift a portion of daytime solar energy to evening and night demand.

- Increase flexible daytime electricity consumption.

- Improve grid reliability, voltage management and forecasting.

- Reduce costly peak-period power procurement where economically justified.

- Create transparent performance measures and investment priorities.

## 4. Recommended Technical Interventions


## 4.1 Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS)

Grid-scale batteries can absorb electricity during solar-rich hours and discharge it when demand rises. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) is one technology option for stationary storage; final selection should compare safety, lifecycle cost, degradation, efficiency, warranty, fire protection and local operating conditions.

| Illustrative pilot parameter | Indicative design |
| --- | --- |
| Battery energy capacity | 100 MWh |
| Power conversion system | 25 MW |
| Nominal charge / discharge duration | 4 hours at 25 MW |
| Battery chemistry | LFP (subject to competitive evaluation) |
| Control and safety | BMS, EMS, thermal management and fire protection |
| Grid connection | Voltage level and transformer rating to be determined by grid |
|   | study |

This is an illustrative pilot concept, not an approved project specification. Detailed sizing must consider the local load curve, solar profile, interconnection constraints, required duration, round-trip efficiency and degradation.

## 4.2 Pumped Hydro Energy Storage (PHES)

Pumped hydro stores energy by using electricity to pump water to an upper reservoir. When electricity is needed, water is released through turbines to generate power. Kerala's terrain and existing reservoir infrastructure make feasibility assessment worthwhile, but each site must be assessed for water availability, geology, ecological impact, land requirements, competing water uses and transmission access.

Illustrative example: a 100 MW pumping load operating for 8 hours consumes 800 MWh. At an assumed 80% round-trip efficiency, approximately 640 MWh would be returned. Actual performance depends on the site and plant design.

## 4.3 Electric-Vehicle Charging

Introduce time-of-day tariffs and smart charging to encourage EV charging during periods of high solar output. Government fleets, electric buses, commercial fleets and public charging hubs can provide predictable daytime demand. Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) should be treated as an optional future capability requiring suitable vehicles, chargers, standards, customer consent and distribution-network approval.

## 4.4 Green Hydrogen

Electrolysers can use renewable electricity to produce hydrogen from water. Hydrogen may serve selected industrial, chemical or transport uses where demand and supply chains are established. It should not be the default destination for all surplus electricity: conversion losses and the cost of electrolysers, storage, transport and end-use equipment can make direct use or battery storage more economical.

## 4.5 Smart Grid and Energy Management

Deploy an Energy Management System (EMS) that combines solar forecasts, demand forecasts, grid constraints, battery state of charge, flexible-load availability and market prices. The EMS should recommend or control dispatch under approved operating rules. AI forecasting can support decisions, but grid protection and real-time control must remain deterministic, secure, auditable and compliant with grid codes.

## 4.6 Industrial and Municipal Load Shifting

Encourage flexible processes to operate during solar-rich hours: water pumping into storage reservoirs, pre-cooling in cold stores, building cooling, selected manufacturing processes and agricultural pumping. Participation should be voluntary or contract-based, with clear measurement and compensation for demand response.


## 4.7 Interstate Power Trading

When local storage and flexible demand are insufficient, KSEB can assess sale of available surplus through bilateral contracts or approved power-market mechanisms. Potential counterparties may include neighbouring states such as Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. Feasibility depends on transmission availability, scheduling rules, market prices, losses and contractual obligations.


## 5. Integrated Operating Model

The proposed operating sequence is: forecast generation and demand; serve current demand; charge storage; activate contracted flexible loads; pump water where a suitable PHES facility is available; offer remaining eligible surplus to the market; and curtail only when required for system security or when no feasible outlet remains.

| Illustrative allocation | Power | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct consumption | 50 MW | Homes, businesses and other current loads |
| Battery charging | 20 MW | Store for later discharge |
| EV charging | 10 MW | Transport energy |
| Pumped-hydro pumping | 15 MW | Store energy as gravitational potential |
| Other flexible loads | 5 MW | Industrial / municipal demand |
| Total | 100 MW | Illustrative allocation only |

The 100 MW example is a conceptual power allocation, not a forecast or a recommended fixed split. The actual allocation must change dynamically with system conditions. The pumped-hydro entry is a pumping load; it is not generation at that time.

## 8. Governance and Institutional Responsibilities

• KSEB: system data, distribution and transmission planning, procurement, storage integration and power trading, subject to applicable approvals.


- State Load Despatch Centre: secure scheduling, forecasting coordination, dispatch and reporting under applicable grid rules.

- ANERT: renewable-energy programme coordination, technical support and stakeholder engagement.

- Kerala State Electricity Regulatory Commission: regulatory approvals, tariff frameworks and consumer-protection oversight within its jurisdiction.

- State departments and local bodies: public-building loads, water systems, transport fleets and local implementation support.

A cross-agency task force should publish a quarterly progress dashboard and an annual review of costs, benefits, curtailment and consumer impacts.

## 10. Monitoring and Performance Indicators

KSEB and the relevant system operator should publish monthly indicators including:

- Solar generation (MU) and hourly generation profile.

- Renewable-energy curtailment (MU), with reason codes.

- Daytime demand served directly by solar generation.

- BESS charging, discharging, availability and round-trip efficiency.

- Pumped-hydro pumping and generation, where applicable.

- EV charging and flexible-load consumption during solar-rich hours.

- Electricity sold, imported and associated market revenue or cost.

- Peak-period procurement and system reliability indicators.

## 11. Risks and Safeguards

The programme should include grid-stability studies, protection coordination, fire and thermal safety standards, cybersecurity controls, transparent procurement, battery recycling and producer-responsibility arrangements, water and biodiversity safeguards for hydro projects, and consumer protections for time-of-day tariffs. Storage dispatch must


not compromise reserves or other essential grid services.

## 12. Final Recommendations

- 1. Begin with a state-wide hourly solar-surplus and curtailment assessment.

- 2. Prioritise competitively procured BESS at substations where studies show a clear need and measurable system value.

- 3. Assess pumped hydro for long-duration storage through site-specific feasibility and environmental studies.

- 4. Introduce smart daytime EV charging and voluntary industrial / municipal demand response.

- 5. Strengthen forecasting, grid visibility and EMS capabilities.

- 6. Use interstate trading for surplus that cannot be stored or absorbed economically.

- 7. Develop green hydrogen only where credible buyers and infrastructure justify the investment.

## 13. Conclusion

Surplus solar electricity should be treated as a valuable energy resource. Kerala can increase the useful share of its renewable generation by combining direct consumption, storage, flexible demand, smart-grid operation and electricity trading. The best policy is not to pursue 100% utilisation at any cost, but to maximise economically and technically feasible utilisation while preserving system security and environmental responsibility.

Policy objective: Generate when sunlight is available, use what is needed immediately, store or convert what can be used later, and trade remaining power when feasible.
